# Lecture 6: Optical Flow & Motion Tracking

> **The hook:** Between two video frames, every pixel moves a little. Optical flow measures that motion — and the math only works where the texture lets it.

<Note title="What We Cover">

- Brightness constancy and the optical flow equation
- Lucas-Kanade: one equation per pixel, least squares per window
- The aperture problem and the eigenvalue test
- Pyramidal LK for large motions
- Dense flow with Farneback, and forward-backward checks

</Note>

---

## Brightness Constancy

Assume a pixel keeps its intensity as it moves: `Ix * u + Iy * v + It = 0`. One equation, two unknowns — a single pixel cannot determine its own motion.

---

## Lucas-Kanade

Assume all pixels in a small window share the same motion. Stack their equations into `A [u, v]^T = b` and solve the normal equations `(A^T A) [u, v]^T = A^T b`. `A^T A` is the same structure tensor that Harris used last week.

```python
ATA = A.T @ A
ATb = A.T @ b
min_eig = np.linalg.eigvalsh(ATA).min()
if min_eig > tau:
    flow = np.linalg.solve(ATA, ATb)
```

---

## The Aperture Problem

On a straight edge, only the motion perpendicular to the edge is observable — `A^T A` has one tiny eigenvalue. Gate on the **minimum** eigenvalue: both directions must be constrained. This is why trackers start from corners.

---

## Pyramids and Validation

LK linearizes, so it only handles small motions. Build an image pyramid, solve at the coarsest level, and refine downward. Then track backward from frame 2 to frame 1; a reliable track returns to where it started.

```python
p1, st, _ = cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK(prev, curr, p0, None, winSize=(21, 21), maxLevel=3)
p0r, st_b, _ = cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK(curr, prev, p1, None)
fb_err = np.linalg.norm(p0 - p0r, axis=-1)
good = (st.ravel() == 1) & (fb_err.ravel() < 1.0)
```

<Warning title="Common Misconception">

Gating on the maximum eigenvalue (an edge passes) or trusting the LK status flag alone. Use the minimum eigenvalue and a forward-backward check.

</Warning>
